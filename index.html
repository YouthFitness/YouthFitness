Training

Simple training principles that actually give you something to track.

The goal is not to make every workout exhausting. The goal is to create a repeatable stimulus and improve it over time.

0 RIR and training to failure

RIR means “reps in reserve.” At 0 RIR, you reach the point where you cannot complete another full rep with good form. That level of effort can be useful because it makes the set highly challenging and can help recruit a large amount of muscle.

But failure is a tool, not a rule for every set. On big compound lifts, stopping a little earlier can make technique and recovery easier. A practical approach is to use very hard sets when the exercise is controlled and safe, while keeping form as the limit.

Remember

0 RIR does not mean sloppy reps. Once technique breaks down, the set is effectively over even if you could force out another ugly rep.

Why two hard sets can be enough to start

Two challenging working sets per exercise can be a practical starting point, especially when the sets are performed with focus and close to your actual limit. More sets are not automatically better if the quality drops or recovery becomes a problem.

Think of two sets as a baseline, not a universal law. Some people will benefit from more weekly volume, and some exercises are easier to recover from than others.

Example

Bench press: 2 working sets after warm-ups. Log the reps and weight. Next time, try to beat your previous performance while keeping your technique consistent.

Nutrition

Calories decide the direction. Food quality helps decide how you feel.

You do not need a perfect diet. You need a calorie target that matches your goal and enough protein, carbohydrates, fats, and micronutrients to support it.

01

Fat loss: calorie deficit

A calorie deficit means eating fewer calories than you use over time. That creates the energy gap needed for body weight to trend downward.

Small, sustainable deficit High protein Keep lifting
02

Muscle gain: calorie surplus

A calorie surplus means eating more energy than you use. A modest surplus can support muscle growth while limiting unnecessary fat gain.

Modest surplus Protein at meals Carbs for training

What to avoid making a habit

“Avoid” does not mean “never eat.” It means do not let these habits crowd out the foods that make your goal easier.

•
Crash diets: huge calorie cuts can make training and recovery harder.
•
All-or-nothing rules: one meal does not ruin a week.
•
Living on ultra-processed snacks: easy to overeat and often low in filling nutrients.
•
Skipping protein: spread protein-rich foods through the day.
•
Ignoring hydration and sleep: nutrition works better when recovery is handled too.

For teens and young athletes, aggressive dieting or weight-cutting should be discussed with a qualified healthcare professional or registered dietitian, especially when still growing.

Training tips

The small details that make a simple program work better.

Warm up for the movement

Use lighter sets to practice the movement and prepare for your working weight. You do not need a marathon warm-up.

Write your lifts down

Record weight, reps, and how hard the set felt. Memory is not a very good progressive-overload system.

Keep form repeatable

A stronger rep is one you can reproduce. Do not change range of motion just to claim extra weight.

Rest long enough

For hard strength or hypertrophy work, give yourself enough rest to produce another quality set.

Recover on purpose

Sleep, food, hydration, and easier days are part of training, not time wasted away from the gym.

Do not copy every trend

A simple routine that you can progress for months beats a complicated routine that you abandon after two weeks.

My rule of thumb

Push the set. Respect the rep.

Training hard matters. Training hard with consistent technique, smart exercise selection, and enough recovery matters more.
